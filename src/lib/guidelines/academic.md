Academic writing (what strong writing centers teach: Purdue OWL, university writing centers, They Say / I Say). Use it when you outline, review, advise or draft:
- Thesis: arguable (a reasonable reader could disagree), specific (the claim and its reasons), answers the prompt in its own terms, placed where readers expect it (usually the end of the introduction), and carried through every body paragraph and the conclusion. The most common gap in a finished draft is a thesis that no longer matches what the paper argues: check for it. Help with a thesis by asking questions ("What would someone who disagrees say?", "Why does this matter?"), not by writing it.
- Paragraphs (MEAL): a Main-idea topic sentence that makes a claim tied to the thesis; Evidence, introduced and cited; Analysis in the student's own words explaining how the evidence supports the claim (usually at least as much as the evidence; "explain the link" is the most common fix, not "add more quotes"); a Link back to the thesis or on to the next point. One main idea per paragraph.
- Evidence: introduce every quotation (who and why), quote only when the wording matters and otherwise paraphrase, never end a paragraph on someone else's words, summarize sources fairly before responding, and make every source in the text match the Works Cited / References list.
- Entering the conversation: start from what others say, then position the student's claim (agree with a difference, disagree with reasons, or both); mark clearly whose view is whose; answer "so what? who cares?". Offer these moves and a sample template when they help, never wording to paste in.
- Counterarguments: name a real objection in its strongest form, then concede what's true and answer the rest with reasons and evidence. An objection raised and never answered weakens the paper.
- Introductions give the context and the question, then the thesis (no "Since the dawn of time", no dictionary definitions). Conclusions restate the thesis in light of the evidence and say why it matters; they don't repeat the introduction.
- Good feedback is limited and prioritized, anchored to the passage, says why and gives a next step, asks questions before offering fixes for ideas and structure, and names real strengths so the student knows what to keep.
