# Video visuals and editing quality

Guidance for Claude when cutting, titling, captioning and generating visuals in a timeline editor. Distilled from a user's own market and production research on short-form explainer and news video (retention psychology, multimedia-learning studies, visual sourcing, motion systems, platform specs). Numbers are starting values from that research. The research labels many of them "practitioner claim" rather than established fact, so treat them as strong defaults, not laws.

**Governing idea:** change something on screen often, but never cut frantically. Every visual must carry meaning. Restraint and consistency are what read as professional; effects and decoration read as amateur.

---

## 1. The quality bar in one paragraph

A professional edit has: motion on frame 1, a clear promise in the first 3 seconds, a visual change every 2–4 s without frantic cutting, one deliberate slow "hold" on the key point, b-roll that depicts what is being said (never decoration), one consistent motion language (same easing, durations and reveal direction everywhere), captions that mirror the narration exactly, text kept inside platform safe zones, clean audio at a consistent loudness, and an ending that closes the loop instead of fading out. Cheap edits have the opposite of each of these: logo stings, every word bouncing, whooshes on every cut, generic posed stock, all-Ken-Burns stills, mismatched clips, and a recap ending.

## 2. Retention structure

### 2.1 The opening (first 10 seconds)
| Time | Must be true |
|---|---|
| 0.0–0.5 s | Something is already moving. No logo, no black, no silence. |
| 0.0–1.5 s | The first 4–8 spoken words name the subject of the promise. A caption is already on screen. A visual cold open (0.5–1.5 s of striking, relevant footage) may come before or under the words. |
| 1.5–3.0 s | The tension, gap or consequence is stated. Visual change #2 has happened. |
| 3–6 s | A credibility stamp: the source, author or evidence named on screen. |
| 6–10 s | A roadmap line ("three things decided this"). By 10 s, 3–4 visual changes have happened. |

- Speech starts within 0.2 s. Audio lagging the picture by more than 200 ms reads as broken.
- **Banned openers:** logo sting or animated intro, greetings ("hey guys, welcome back"), throat-clearing context before the hook, a static first frame. These are the biggest early drop-off causes.
- **Strong hook types:** stakes/consequence for the viewer; contradiction (two facts that seem incompatible); a named gap; a concrete, sourced number; a direct question the video answers; "you've probably heard X" (stated fairly); timeline compression ("three weeks ago… this morning…").
- **Banned hook types:** outrage, fake urgency or countdowns, bait-and-switch, vague "this isn't what you think" on its own.
- **Promise rule:** one promise per video, paid off by about 60% of runtime in words that echo the hook. A hook that doesn't pay off damages trust in the whole channel, not just that video.

### 2.2 Holding attention
- **Re-hook every 15–20 s.** Each section ends by resolving its micro-question and opening the next ("That's the official reason. The other one is stranger."). For 60 s: beats near 0:15, 0:35, then the loop-back near 0:52. For 120 s: about every 20 s. For 180 s: every 18–20 s plus a midpoint stakes reset.
- **Pattern interrupt at each re-hook:** a brief silence, a colour shift, a full-frame text card, sudden stillness, or a switch in visual type. Tie the visual-type switch (footage → map → chart → footage) to the re-hook timestamps, so novelty and re-hooking run on one clock.
- **Section boundaries** are marked by all three: an audio change, a change in visual type, and a spoken signpost ("Here's the part that matters." / "Now the other side." / "What we don't know:").
- Avoid signposts that cue an exit: "so anyway", "as I said", "to recap", "in conclusion".
- Close every gap you open, inside the same video.

### 2.3 Pacing numbers
| Parameter | Default |
|---|---|
| Visual change | Every ~2–4 s (acceptable 2–5 s). A "change" is a new clip, a graphic, a push-in or pan, or a text or chart reveal. It does not have to be a cut. |
| Mean shot length | 2.5–4.0 s for explainers. Fast cutting (~2.4 s average) raises attention but lowers comprehension. Slow cutting (~5.9 s) helps comprehension but loses attention. Feature films average ~3–4 s. |
| Max static | No frame more than 4–5 s without motion. |
| Comprehension hold | Exactly one 4–6 s low-motion hold on the single most important claim, preceded by 0.3–0.6 s of silence, with narration slowed to ~120–130 wpm. Make it a graphic settling (a chart finishing its draw, a card resolving), not frozen footage, which looks like buffering. |
| Narration | 150–165 wpm overall. Hook slightly faster. |
| Punch-in/zoom for emphasis | At most ~1 per 15 s, only on a spoken key word. |
| Simultaneous novel streams | At most 3 at once (narration, on-screen graphic text, new footage, music change, SFX). A caption that matches the speech counts as half. Going past this is the most common cause of a mid-video drop-off. |
| New terms | At most 1 new term per 8 s. Each defined within 3 s, on screen. |
| Numbers | At most ~4 per minute. Each shown on screen when spoken, each with a comparison anchor. |

### 2.4 Endings
- **Default: loop back.** The final line answers the opening question, and the final frame visually rhymes with frame 1 so the video replays smoothly. Alternatives: a forward-looking unknown ("the thing to watch is…") or a hard stop on the strongest line.
- **Never:** a recap, an outro card, a "like and subscribe" end screen, a fade to black, or a logo. Put calls to action in the description or pinned comment.
- In the last 10 s, add no new information, use no recap wording and show no branding.

## 3. Visual sourcing and b-roll

### 3.1 The coherence rule
The strongest single finding in multimedia learning (Mayer's coherence principle: 23 of 23 tests, median effect size ~0.86) is that **removing decorative visuals, sounds and "cool" graphics improves understanding.** So:
- **Every clip depicts what is being said.** No decorative b-roll. If you can't find a clip that depicts the point, use a clean graphic or text card instead.
- **Every juxtaposition is a claim** (the Kuleshov effect). An image under a sentence argues something. Neutral footage of a person cut next to a disaster clip makes an argument nobody said. Never place footage of an identifiable person within one beat of a negative claim about someone else.
- Each beat should have a one-line reason for its visual. If you can't state one, the visual is decoration.

### 3.2 Footage or graphic?
| The beat carries… | Use |
|---|---|
| A concrete, physical, generic subject (weather, a landmark, machinery running, a crowd, a place) | **Real footage.** Its realism adds trust and gives the eye a low-effort beat between dense ones. |
| A number, timeline, comparison, citation or quote | **Motion graphic.** A clip can't show a number honestly. |
| The comprehension hold | **Motion graphic**, almost always. |
| A specific recent event or a specific named thing that stock can't truly show | **Graphic, document card or screen recording of the actual source.** Never a stock stand-in dressed up to look like footage of the real thing. |

On dense, concept-heavy pieces, push graphics to 30–40% of beats and keep real footage for the cold open and one or two scene-setting beats. A graphic's own internal animation meets the visual-change rate without adding a new scene to decode. Default mix otherwise: at least 50% real motion footage, at most 35% stills with Ken Burns, at least 15% motion graphics, and at least one motion graphic every 20 s.

### 3.3 Finding footage that looks premium
- **Never search the abstract noun.** "Inflation" returns nothing usable. "Empty supermarket shelf", "checkout scanner close-up" and "grocery receipt" do. Break every beat into 3–5 concrete, photographable visual nouns plus a one-sentence statement of visual intent.
- **Split named subjects from generic ones.** Named landmarks, specific objects and institutions come from encyclopedic or archival sources (public-domain archives, agency media libraries). Generic actions and objects come from stock libraries. Combine them in the edit.
- **Try several phrasings.** Stock search is keyword matching, not semantic. "Wildfire evacuation" fails, but "wildfire smoke", "highway traffic jam" and "packed car trunk" each succeed, and they cut together into the same beat.
- **Review candidates visually** (a contact sheet of frames at 10/50/90%) before choosing. That's how you catch "technically matches, editorially wrong", like the wrong country's flag.
- Prefer natively vertical footage, or footage at least 1080 px tall after cropping. Upscale or denoise only legitimately sourced material.

### 3.4 What makes stock look cheap
1. **Posed "stock grammar"** is the most recognisable tell: the concerned face, the handshake, typing at a laptop, glossy lighting, staged smiles, a gavel clipart photo. **If the only candidates are posed shots, use no stock at all** and use a graphic or text card instead.
2. **Register mismatch:** commercial lifestyle gloss under a serious subject.
3. **Repetition:** the same clip reused within a piece or across recent pieces. No two adjacent beats share an asset, and don't reuse a clip within ~30 days on a series.
4. **High drama:** shocked faces, chaotic framing, harsh dramatic light, slow-motion intensity. It oversells urgency and reads as unreliable.
5. **All Ken Burns:** a video made of slowly zooming photos is the top "auto-generated" tell.

**Prefer:** observational, naturally lit, non-posed footage of real places, objects and processes (actual weather, actual landmarks, machinery operating). It should look like it belongs in a documentary, not a stock catalogue.

**Always:** strip the stock clip's own audio on import. Keep a licence record for every asset. Never put AI imagery that looks real in place of real people, places or events.

### 3.5 Stills and Ken Burns
- Scale 1.00 → 1.05–1.08 over the shot. More than 8% reads as a zoom effect, not a camera move.
- Vary the direction across the video (push-in, pull-out, pan left, pan up, diagonal). Never the same direction twice in a row.
- Always ease in and out, never linear. Duration 2.5–5 s. Under 2 s the motion can't be perceived.
- Use on at most ~35% of beats.
- For depth, use a parallax move: separate subject from background and move them at different rates.

## 4. Motion graphics and kinetic type

### 4.1 One motion language
Write the motion system down once and apply it everywhere: same easing, same durations, same reveal direction. A viewer reads fast, jarring motion as alarm even when the narration is calm. **Every motion choice is a tone choice.**

| Token | Value | Use |
|---|---|---|
| ease-standard | cubic-bezier(0.2, 0, 0, 1) | entrances, reveals |
| ease-exit | cubic-bezier(0.4, 0, 1, 1) | exits (accelerate away) |
| dur-fast | ~160 ms | dots, chips, small markers |
| dur-base | ~280 ms in / ~200 ms out | cards, lower thirds |
| dur-slow | 400–600 ms (maps/paths 500–800 ms) | charts, maps, timelines |
| stagger | ~60 ms between items, at most 5 items per group | lists, bars |

- **Never linear, never elastic or overshoot or bounce.** No shake, glitch, whoosh-zoom, zoom-punch, flash, or slow-motion emphasis.
- Snap every duration to whole frames (at 30 fps, steps of 33.3 ms).
- **Reveal in reading order:** left to right, top to bottom. Paths and markers draw in the reading direction.
- Charts draw from the baseline. Lines draw along their length. Numbers count up only when the counting is itself the point.
- Exits are faster than entrances (about 70%).
- **One accent per scene.** The accent colour marks the one thing to look at. Nothing else animates in the accent.
- Text highlight is a colour or brightness change only (0–80 ms). No scaling, no bouncing.
- Any recurring decorative element (an arc, a rule, a dot) draws once and stops. It never loops, spins or pulses. Something moving in a loop reads as a loading spinner.
- Light and dark versions must match exactly in timing and layout. Only the colours swap.
- When adding a new kind of animated element, define its motion in the system first so the language doesn't drift.

### 4.2 Kinetic typography
- Use it only for: the hook line, numbers, proper nouns, the key claim, and quoted text. Never for every word. Kinetic type everywhere becomes a competing text stream and looks templated.
- A full-frame text card works well as a pattern interrupt at a re-hook. Hold it long enough to read twice (about 0.3 s per word plus 1 s, minimum 1.5 s).
- Hierarchy: one dominant line, at most one supporting line. Use weight and size contrast, not colour noise. Reserve bold for real emphasis (a key number, a name on first mention). Bold everywhere reads as shouting; bold nowhere reads as flat.
- Sentence case, not ALL CAPS shouting. Never squeeze, condense or letter-space text to make it fit. Step down a defined type scale or break the line.

### 4.3 The component kit
Build a small, reusable set and re-prop it, rather than inventing one-off graphics for each video. Inconsistency reads as amateur.
| Component | Standard |
|---|---|
| **Lower third / name card** | One thin rule or accent bar plus two type sizes (e.g. title 48 px, deck 32 px on 1080 wide). Plate in a surface colour, ink text. Slides in from the left edge plus a fade, ~280 ms. On screen at least 1.5 s (better 3–4 s). Appears at the moment of the claim. |
| **Source card** | Text only: "Source: Name, Month Day Year" plus a one-line claim. No outlet logos or ratings. Type labels in small caps or mono (PRIMARY DOCUMENT / REPORTED BY / ESTIMATE). |
| **Chart** | Re-plot from the source data, never a screenshot of someone's figure. One accent for the subject series, neutral greys for context, direct labels instead of legends, axes labelled, source shown on the card. Never tell series apart by hue alone, and never use a red/green pair as the only difference. |
| **Map** | Clean vector base (public-domain sources such as Natural Earth). The marker or route draws in over 500–800 ms. Locator inset for orientation. |
| **Timeline** | A horizontal line, a dot per event, one highlighted. The default answer to "decided then, takes effect later". |
| **Quote card** | Short quotes (under ~25 words), attributed and dated. |
| **Comparison / before-after split** | For "what changed". |
| **Known / unknown card** | A recurring card for what isn't known yet. Recurring named segments build recognition. |

Rotate between a few motion-graphic families across a series to avoid habituation, while keeping the brand constants (type, colour, easing) fixed.

### 4.4 Branding inside the video
- No logo animation, sting, intro or outro card. If there's a corner bug, it's static, small (~56 px tall on 1080 × 1920), 70–85% opacity, inside the safe zone, and fades in after the hook (1.5–3 s) over 200–300 ms. Provide a solid version and a knockout version for busy footage.
- Brand recognition comes from consistent caption style, type, colour logic and components, not from logo time.

## 5. Captions

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Coverage | 100% of narration, burned in. Built by aligning to the known script, not transcribing. |
| Content | Word-for-word what's said. Never a paraphrase. A second, different text stream on screen at the same time forces two reads and hurts comprehension. |
| Chunking | At most 2 lines × ~5 words. 1–3 words per page on hook or punchy beats, 4–6 on explanatory beats. Break at phrase boundaries, never between an article and its noun. |
| Size | About 60–75 px cap height on 1080 × 1920 (≈64–65 px is a familiar default). Floor ~48–55 px, ceiling ~100 px before it starts covering the picture. On 16:9, scale proportionally (about 5–6% of frame height). |
| Font | A heavy or semibold grotesque or humanist sans, tight tracking, no italics. |
| Contrast | Never rely on the footage being dark. Use either a solid or ≥70–85% opacity dark plate, or white fill with a 4–8 px dark outline plus a soft shadow. Measure contrast against the plate or local background: at least 4.5:1, aim for broadcast-level 7:1 and up. Soft off-white on deep navy or charcoal is easier on the eye than pure white on pure black. |
| Position | Lower-centre, inside the safe box, above the platform UI. On vertical, roughly 60–68% down the frame (around y 1150–1250 of 1920). Keep it out of the bottom ~35% and the right ~120–140 px. |
| Active-word highlight | Change colour or brightness only (e.g. active word at full ink, others muted). **Never scale, pop or bounce words.** The "every word bounces and scales" preset is the most recognisable auto-editor look. |
| Emphasis | Highlight at most ~3 key terms per video (numbers, names, the key claim word). Overuse cancels the signal. |
| Timing | Each word appears on, or up to 80 ms before, the spoken word. Never after. |
| Hygiene | Proofread numbers, names and quotes against the script. A typo on a factual term undermines credibility. Never let a lone punctuation mark (such as a dash) become its own caption. Strip stray symbols. |

Captions are always correct to include: viewers often watch muted, and captions help with accents, technical words and second-language viewers. But keep them identical to the speech, and don't stack captions, a different headline and a lower third, all new, at the same time.

## 6. Colour and grade

- **One palette for the whole piece**, defined as tokens (background, surface, ink, muted ink, rule, and one accent) and used for every graphic, card, caption and chart. Any new colour has to fit that register.
- **Muted and warm-neutral reads as reputable.** Prefer a deep navy or charcoal over pure #000, and a warm off-white over pure #FFF. Save higher saturation for things that are truly urgent, never as the default way to stand out. Red or black urgency palettes and "BREAKING"-style red read as alarm or tabloid.
- **Monochrome plus brightness contrast** is a fully professional option (it reads premium and editorial). A single restrained accent hue is the other.
- **Colour-blind safety:** separate by lightness as well as hue. Use a colour-blind-safe set (e.g. Okabe-Ito) for more than 2 categories, and label directly.
- **Grade footage to match** so clips from different sources sit together: similar white balance, contrast and saturation across adjacent shots. Calm, natural, even light. If you darken or desaturate b-roll to keep text readable, do it consistently (e.g. 25–45% under text).
- **Look consistent across a series.** A channel that looks slightly different every video reads as amateur, however good each one is. Before finishing, compare against recent pieces. If it would look out of place next to them, that's a flag.

## 7. Transitions

- **The default is a cut.** Most transitions should be invisible: straight cuts, cuts on action, cuts on the breath or beat.
- **J-cut** (next section's audio starts under the current picture) to pull the viewer across a section boundary. **L-cut** (audio carries over a new picture) to change the visual without breaking a sentence. This is the cleanest way to hit a 2–3 s visual rate without chopping narration. Bridge section boundaries with audio overlap, not hard audio cuts.
- **Match cut** (shared shape, motion or position) to link two ideas cheaply and elegantly.
- **Montage** to compress time or scale: no more than 4 shots.
- A slow push-in counts as a "change". Use it on held explanatory beats instead of cutting.
- Short cross-dissolves (8–12 frames) only for time passing or a soft change of scene. A graphic wipe only if it's part of the defined motion system (e.g. a brand rule that grows to width).
- **Avoid:** star wipes, spins, cube flips, page curls, glitch or RGB-split, light-leak packs as a style, zoom-blur whooshes, flashes, shake. These are the clearest signs of a template edit.
- **SFX discipline:** whoosh, riser or hit only to mark a real change of direction (topic shift, reveal, entering b-roll), at most about 1 per 15 s. If every cut has a whoosh, the mix becomes predictable and crowded.

## 8. Explaining ideas visually

- **One idea per beat, simple to complex, small steps.** The viewer should always know which part of the explanation they're in. Mark each new unit with a visual-type change plus a short pause (~0.5 s).
- **Preview the structure** ("three things"), then number on screen as you go ("1 of 3"). Signalling reduces mental effort.
- **Thought-experiment sequence:** "Say you [concrete starting point, with a number]. [One step.] Now change one thing. [Result.]" Animate it as a step reveal: at most 3 steps, one variable changing per step, every number in the chain on screen.
- **Factor ladder for scale:** a familiar unit, then ×10, then ×10 again, then "the figure in this story is n of those". Animate it as repeated identical units stacking or zooming out. It works because each step can be checked.
- **Scale comparisons that hold up:** per-person division, a rate with a denominator ("14 in every 100,000"), share of a familiar whole, a neutral physical object, time equivalence. Pick neutral comparison objects, and say "roughly" when approximating.
- **Show numbers properly:** say "about 240 million", show "$239.7m" on screen. Always show direction and baseline ("up from", "compared with"), a date, and a denominator. Show risk as before → after in absolute terms plus the relative change. Never a lone "+40%". One figure per sentence and per card.
- **Prediction beat:** pose a real question, hold ~1.5 s on a clean card, then reveal. One per video.
- **"Here's the chart, now watch it change":** draw the baseline state, hold, then animate the change the narration describes. Put the source under the chart.
- **Diagrams:** draw paths and arrows in the order of the process, label directly, dim (to ~30–40% opacity) the parts not being discussed, and highlight the active part in the accent.
- **Simplify honestly:** you may round a magnitude, but never reverse a direction or drop an uncertainty range. If a graphic simplifies, it can carry a small "simplified" or "approximate" label.

## 9. Platform specs and safe zones

These values come from secondary sources and change often. Design to the conservative box and check against real app screenshots.

| Format | Size | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical short (Shorts/Reels/TikTok) | 1080 × 1920, 9:16, 30 fps | Shorts up to 3:00. Reels: keep under ~90 s for cross-posting safety. Explainers usually 60–150 s. |
| Landscape video | 1920 × 1080 or 3840 × 2160, 16:9 | Standard broadcast practice: keep text inside ~90% title-safe and action inside ~93% action-safe. |
| Instagram grid crop of a vertical cover | centre 1080 × 1440 (3:4) | Keep the title and mark inside the centre 1440 px; ~240 px is cut top and bottom. |

**Vertical UI zones (1080 × 1920):**
- Conservative box for all burned-in text: **x 120–960, y ~270–1250.** Platform UI covers roughly the top 240–270 px, the bottom ~35% (~670 px on Meta) and the right 120–140 px (TikTok action rail).
- A looser practitioner box is a centred ~900 × 1160 (conservative) to 900 × 1400 px.
- Reported per platform: TikTok sides ~120, top ~240, bottom ~480–660. Reels top 14% (269), sides 6% (65), bottom 35% (672). Shorts: left ~60, right ~120, top 180–380, bottom ~380–390. When sources disagree, use the largest.
- Footage can fill the whole frame. Only text, faces that matter and key graphics need to stay inside the box.

**Export:** no platform watermarks, correct aspect ratio, no burned-in platform UI, a separate clean export for each platform.

## 10. Audio-visual sync

- **Cut on the breath or the beat, not on an arbitrary frame.** It feels intentional and less choppy.
- Visual events (a card appearing, a chart drawing, a highlight) land on the spoken word they illustrate, or up to ~80 ms before it. Never late.
- **Silence:** one 0.3–0.6 s gap before the key claim. No dead air longer than ~0.8 s anywhere else. Never true digital silence inside speech: keep room tone under every gap.
- **Music:** optional, low, ducked 9–18 dB under speech. It can change at section boundaries. A dropout of about 1 s before a major line works as an audio reset. Calm, low-key sound design suits informational content; a constant hype bed costs credibility.
- **Loudness:** master to about −14 LUFS integrated, −1 dBTP. No clipping, no room echo, consistent level across clips. Poor audio makes identical content seem less credible, so audio quality is part of quality, not polish.
- Tension build (rising music plus shortening shots into a reveal, then a dropout) only when the reveal really is significant.

## 11. Making generated visuals look good

This applies to title cards, motion-graphic scenes, SVG illustrations, backgrounds and generated stills. The goal is "designed", not "made of shapes".

### 11.1 What reads as amateur geometric
- Flat primitives floating on a flat background with no relationship to each other or to a grid (a circle, a square, a triangle "for interest").
- Default rainbow or high-saturation colours, several accents competing, pure #000 or #FFF.
- Centred everything, with equal-weight elements and no focal point.
- Generic "tech" clichés: glowing orbs, gradient blobs, sparkles, glassmorphism panels, lens flares, starfields, globe-with-swoosh, circuit lines, hexagon grids. These read as "software template", not "editorial".
- Drop shadows and bevels on everything, outer glows, and heavy gradients on marks.
- Default font at default tracking, ALL CAPS, more than two typefaces.
- Shapes that loop, spin or pulse forever.
- Icons or illustrations in mixed styles (different stroke weights, corner logic and fill styles in one frame).

### 11.2 What reads as professional
1. **Start from a grid.** Margins (e.g. 72 px on 1080 wide, or ~6–7% of the short side), a column grid (6 columns, 24 px gutters on vertical), and an 8 px spacing scale (8/16/24/32/48/64). Align every element to it. Asymmetric layouts on a grid look designed; centred stacks without a grid look default.
2. **Build layered depth.** Three planes: (a) background: a deep tone with subtle texture (fine grain or noise at 2–4%, a soft radial light falloff or vignette, a large cropped brand shape or photo at 8–10% opacity); (b) midground: the content (diagram, chart, image) with a clear edge or plate; (c) foreground: type and the one accent element. In motion, move the planes at slightly different rates (parallax) and keep the background almost still.
3. **Add texture and light, not effects.** Gentle grain, soft shadows that are consistent and low-opacity (one light direction), slight tonal variation inside large fills instead of perfectly flat colour, and a darkening or desaturation overlay on photos under text (25–45%). Avoid strong glows, chrome or 3D bevels.
4. **Typography carries the design.** One family, two or three weights, tight tracking on display sizes (−2% to −4%), generous line height on body. Pair a large confident headline with small, quiet supporting labels: small caps or mono "dateline" micro-type ("UPDATED 09:41", "SOURCE") signals rigour without shapes. Hairline rules (1–2 px) separate and structure the layout.
5. **Restraint: one idea, one accent, one focal point.** Sleekness comes from subtraction. Let one structural device recur everywhere (e.g. a thin rule or bar that is also the lower-third edge and the divider) so the system feels intentional. Use one corner-radius family and one icon style (e.g. 2 px stroke at 48 px, round caps, single colour).
6. **Make it specific.** Prefer a diagram of the actual mechanism, a re-plotted real chart, a real map or a document excerpt over abstract decoration. A visual that could sit under any script is decoration.
7. **Stylised, labelled illustration** is fine for concepts (how a process works), but keep it visibly illustrative and never photoreal for real people, places or events.
8. **Motion finish:** apply the easing tokens, stagger group entrances by ~60 ms, let elements settle and then rest. Stillness after a reveal reads as confidence.
9. **Check at real size:** view at phone scale, in grayscale (does the hierarchy survive?), and with a slight blur (is the focal point still obvious?). Check contrast of text against its actual local background, not the average.

### 11.3 Title card recipe
Deep neutral background with subtle grain → a large cropped brand shape or a darkened photo at low opacity, off-centre → a small mono or small-caps label (segment or date) → a headline of 2–6 words in a heavy weight, sentence case, tightly tracked, aligned to the grid → one accent element (a rule, a single highlighted word, or a dot). Entrance: label fades in, rule draws (~300 ms), headline rises 12–24 px with ease-standard (~280 ms) at a 60 ms stagger, then holds still.

## 12. Quality checklist (run before export)

**Opening and structure**
- [ ] Frame 1 is moving. Caption on frame 1. Speech within 0.2 s. No logo, intro or greeting.
- [ ] Promise stated in the first 3 s and paid off by ~60%, in echoing words.
- [ ] Source or credibility stamp on screen by ~6 s. Structure previewed by ~10 s.
- [ ] Re-hook plus pattern interrupt every 15–20 s. Every gap opened is closed.
- [ ] Ending loops back or names what's coming next. No recap, outro card, CTA or fade. Last frame rhymes with the first.

**Picture**
- [ ] Mean shot length 2.5–4 s. Nothing static over 4–5 s. Exactly one 4–6 s comprehension hold, done as a graphic.
- [ ] Every clip depicts what's said. No decorative or posed stock. No juxtaposition that implies an unstated claim.
- [ ] Numbers, comparisons and specific claims are graphics, not stock. At least one motion graphic every 20 s.
- [ ] No adjacent repeated assets. Ken Burns at most 8%, direction varied, at most ~35% of beats.
- [ ] At most 3 novel information streams at any moment.
- [ ] Footage graded to match. One palette. One accent per scene.

**Motion and type**
- [ ] One easing set, no linear, bounce or elastic. Durations from the token set. Reveals in reading order.
- [ ] Kinetic type only on hook, numbers, names, key claim and quotes.
- [ ] Transitions are mostly cuts and J/L-cuts. No template transitions. SFX only at changes of direction.
- [ ] Graphics are layered, on a grid, specific, and restrained (section 11).

**Captions and text**
- [ ] Captions cover 100% of narration, verbatim, ≤2 lines × ~5 words, readable plate or stroke, highlight by colour only, ≤80 ms lead.
- [ ] Numbers, names and quotes proofread. No orphan punctuation captions.
